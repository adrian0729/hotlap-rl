What the sheet shows

The black car is driven by a neural network. It learned on computer-generated road, and these laps are the first time it has met a real circuit.

The purple car drives the optimal lap: the minimum lap time for the same car on the same physics, found by an optimal-control solver. Every lap here is measured against it.

The small circles on the road edges are all the network sees of the road: forty points on each edge, reaching 150 to 500 m ahead depending on speed. A run ends the moment the car's centre crosses the dashed line into the last metre of road.

On the plan, a red spike at each corner group shows the time lost there. Corner groups are runs of corners with no straight between them, numbered from the start line; they are not official turn numbers. Time lost on a straight is charged to the corner before it.

Both targets were set before the test and both were missed. The rest of this page is what that miss taught: where the policy fails, why, and what fixed most of it.

The driver, and how it learned

The driver is a small neural network. 222 numbers go in, they pass through three layers of 256 units, and two commands come out: how far to turn the wheel and how hard to press the pedal. In between are 189,186 learned numbers, and those numbers are the whole driver.

It is told only what the first group shows: forty points on each edge of the road ahead, sixteen things the car can feel, and six numbers that describe the car. It is never told which circuit it is on, or how far round the lap it is.

It learned by trial and error. 2,048 simulated cars drove at once on computer-generated strips of road, with every command nudged at random so that new things were tried. A second network, the critic, predicted how much reward each moment would bring. Commands that turned out better than the critic expected were made a little more likely, and the others a little less.

That update ran 7,629 times, a billion decisions in all: a year and seven months of driving, simulated in two and a quarter hours. On the laps above, none of the training machinery runs. The network drives alone, with the same numbers on all twelve circuits.

The held-out test

Five networks were trained with one recipe and five random seeds, a billion simulation steps each. Twelve circuits were locked away from the first day of the project. The test plan and its pass marks were written down and tagged in the repository before any network touched those circuits, and the test ran once: ten laps per network per circuit.

Mirrored and reversed versions of the twelve circuits gave the same picture: 8 of 12 completed at the median, with gaps of 5.3% and 5.6%. Every one of the 550 crashes in the test was the car leaving the road. None was a spin or a stall.

Why it leaves the road

The obvious guess was that the policy arrives at tight corners too fast. A series of studies, each planned before its data existed, said otherwise.

It is not the braking. On controlled test corners, all nine networks studied entered within about 10% of the speed their own optimum would use, and their braking distance scaled with speed the way the physics does. Arriving too fast after a long straight was the failure the project expected. It was not seen once.

It is the inside edge. Of 160 crashes on real development circuits, 159 left the road on the inside of the corner, and 146 of them where the inside edge had moved by a metre or more in the last 100 m. Generated training tracks almost never do that: under 0.5% of their tight corners, against 79% on the real crash corners.

The line follows a slow change and ignores a sharp one. Move the inside edge of a whole corner by a metre and the network's line moves about three quarters of a metre with it. Squeeze the same metre into a few metres of road and the line barely reacts. The real edge changes at its crash corners are that sharp.

More training did not help. More room did.

Training on real track-width profiles helped. Training five times longer did not. What worked was ending training runs 1.4 m from the edge instead of 1.0 m, so the network learns to keep more clearance. Racing is still judged at 1.0 m.

The margin is a dial. Each extra 0.2 m buys clearance at the corners where the policy used to crash and costs about half a point of lap time. It does not teach the policy to follow the edge any better. It simply stops driving so close to it.

These are the twelve development circuits, whose width profiles the track generator borrows, so they are not an independent test. The margin recipe has not been run on the twelve held-out circuits.

How it was built and checked

One set of vehicle equations is compiled twice: for the simulator the policy trains in, and for the optimal-control solver. The optimum is therefore exact for the problem the policy actually faces, and the simulator reproduces the solver's laps to within 0.02%. The policy sees the road edges 150 to 500 m ahead, its own motion and the car's parameters. It never sees its position on the lap.

Nine studies were pre-registered. For each, the design, the code and the decision rules were frozen under a git tag before any result existed, and the deciding analysis ran once.

About 400 commits and more than 2,000 tests. Everything on this page is computed from result files in the repository. The scoreboard is the pre-registered analysis as it was written. The replays are the registered runs simulated again for this page, which the project's own rules label post hoc; each reproduces the stored result.

Three smaller results

A good critic is not a good gradient

The critics predict their own returns with R² of 0.80 to 0.93, yet the gradient of that value explains almost none of the true price of the car's state. The price can be decoded from what the network sees. The critic's gradient just does not express it.

Curriculum scores do not rank regret

Curriculum methods replay the levels a score says matter most. Measured against exact regret on mazes, none of three standard scores ranked levels better than the agent's own return did.

The public F1 feed has no racing line

Across 85 qualifying sessions, the public position data carries no driver-specific line: the between-driver signal is zero, while a planted 0.5 m offset is found at 93 to 100% of stations.
